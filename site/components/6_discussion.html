5. Discussion

The combined MGEX receiver-clock product shows a distance-dependent structure with $$\lambda = 1862$$ km. The estimate remains within 1,819–2,000 km when the minimum baseline is raised from 50 to 2,000 km, while the binned-fit R² improves from 0.70 to about 0.92. It persists on quiet days and after distance matching, and it survives the two computed mundane channels rather than only label-permutation nulls: the forward-modelled shared satellite-error kernel is flat through the signal band and decays an order of magnitude more slowly than the measured profile; conditioning the same channel on the product's own ephemerides and satellite-clock content decays no faster than λ = 6,650 km (~3.6× the measured scale; Paper 1, Step 5.2); and the weather-organized partitions return the opposite of the atmospheric signature — cross-hemisphere pairs, which share no synoptic weather, carry the cleanest decay, and same-latitude-band pairs beyond 8,000 km return negative coherence where a zonal atmospheric kernel requires positive coherence. The ±112 km formal fit uncertainty treats 1.83 million pair-days as independent; cluster-robust resampling of whole epochs inflates the statistical error only modestly — ±86 km under day-block bootstrap, ±155 km under month-block bootstrap, and ±94 km under leave-one-month-out jackknife — so the within-product statistical precision is about ±8%. The dominant scale uncertainties are systematic rather than statistical: single-window realisations are epoch-dependent at the ~30–40% level, and the matched-day cross-center comparison contributes ~±600 km. A factorial decomposition on identical station-days (Step 3.5, §4.5b) shows that the estimator axes — Welch segment length and per-epoch common-mode removal — move the fitted scale by ≲±25%, so the residual offset to the IGS-final family (3,330–4,549 km, Papers 1–2) resides in the clock products rather than in the correlation estimator, and the same band-ordered profile (longest scale in the lowest resolved sub-band) is recovered in both product families. The amplitude is conditioned the same way and more strongly: the fitted A is a bounded, saturating phase-direction index (§4.5b, Step 3.6) whose ~7× offset from the IGS-final family decomposes into a ~3× statistic nonlinearity, the documented estimator axes, and a product-level difference in the shared in-phase budget — the linear coherence measured on identical pair-days is A = 0.250, and the deeper negative MGEX asymptote is the signature of its own zero-sum datum, not a sign discrepancy. The result is nonetheless a useful cross-product corroboration of a correlation scale in the thousands-of- kilometres range, not a direct measurement-equivalence claim with the earlier GPS PPP products.

The conventional directional measures do not replicate robustly. The full-range EW/NS ratio is 1.29 but is non-significant under station-clustered bootstrap (p = 0.49). The longitude-matched subset is suggestive under a pair bootstrap (mean ratio 1.71, p = 0.058) yet non-significant under spatially clustered resampling (p = 0.214). Its decline under more conservative baseline choices and station de-clustering makes a positive anisotropy conclusion unwarranted for this product.

The orbital finding is more informative but has a narrower evidential status. The primary monthly $$\lambda$$ and EW/NS estimators are null after Bonferroni correction, unsurprising with only 16 complete chronological months and unstable monthly exponential fits. The model-free PA-difference statistic, monthly mean EW minus NS phase alignment, has r = −0.716 with orbital speed (p = 0.0018) and r = −0.767 with velocity projected onto the CMB dipole (p = 0.0005). Because the nominal p-values assume independent monthly points, they are reported alongside autocorrelation-aware diagnostics: effective- degrees-of-freedom values p = 0.072 and p = 0.018, matched AR(1)-surrogate values p = 0.019 and p = 0.003, and circular-shift values p = 0.125 and p = 0.063 (the 1/16 floor for the projection channel). The association is therefore tempered but not erased, and it is the physically motivated velocity-projection channel that survives; leave-one-month-out correlations remain within r = −0.66 to −0.86, excluding single-month dominance. These signs and magnitudes are consistent with the preceding TEP-GNSS analyses, which motivated the physical prediction before this MGEX analysis. That history makes the result corroborative across products. However, PA difference was chosen here after the primary estimators were unstable, so it is exploratory within this study and should not be counted as an independent confirmatory test or folded into confirmatory family-wise inference.

The corrected CMB analysis keeps January 2025 through April 2026 as separate months. Its direct CMB-projection correlation is r = −0.733, consistent with the stronger weighted orbital result (r = −0.767, p = 0.0005). The full-sky scan has a nominal maximum at RA = 230°, Dec = +60° (r = −0.745), 82.7° from the CMB dipole. That angular separation must not be interpreted as a failed CMB test. Earth’s orbital velocity remains in the ecliptic plane, so dot-product regression identifies only an axis’s in-plane projection; directions separated substantially in three dimensions can give nearly identical annual predictors. Here the CMB and nominal-best projected predictors correlate at r = 0.984, and 61 of 703 grid directions equal or exceed the direct-CMB |r|. The 30° three-dimensional rule is thus not an identifiable criterion for this design. The permutation result (plus-one p = 0.005 over 200 complete-grid shuffles) supports directional annual structure, while the direct CMB projection remains consistent with that structure. The 60° sensitivity result and daily analysis are corroborative diagnostics, not independent confirmations. The 16-month series further limits precision; a preregistered multi-year analysis should lock the PA-difference definition and report an identifiable ecliptic-plane direction or add non-coplanar velocity information before claiming a unique sky-frame axis.

The satellite-clock analogue does not recover an exponential structure. GLONASS, BeiDou, and Galileo have negative fitted R² values, while GPS has R² = +0.13 with boundary convergence. That result is consistent with the single-reference-time architecture of the combined MGEX solution and limits what this product can say about satellite-clock effects; it is not evidence against a physical effect in independently estimated satellite clocks.

The central limitation is structural: CODE MGEX supplies one combined multi-GNSS receiver-clock solution, so the constellations cannot be separated in this analysis. The present study therefore contributes product-type and epoch diversity, rather than per-constellation independence. Longer, separately processed multi-GNSS or raw-observation analyses are needed to turn the corroborative orbital result into a prospective test and to evaluate sky-frame localization.
